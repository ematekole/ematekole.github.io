**Funding**: Department of Defense – Aligning Dosimetry and Biomarkers of Lung Injury with Prophylaxis and Mitigation of Damage from Radionuclides and Metals
**Period of Performance**: Oct. 1, 2021-Sept. 30, 2025

Military personnel, Warfighters, and first responders may encounter airborne contaminants during deployment in a wide range of operational environments. These exposures can result in respiratory injuries that appear either shortly after exposure, such as pneumonitis (inflammation of lung tissue), or develop over time into chronic conditions like pulmonary fibrosis (scarring of lung tissue). Contaminants may originate from radioactive particles released during nuclear power plant accidents, nuclear weapon events, or radiological dispersal devices ("dirty bombs"). Non-radioactive contaminants, including toxic metals such as tungsten alloys and depleted uranium used in military munitions, can also pose significant health risks when inhaled.

This project aims to develop realistic contamination source models for both radioactive and heavy metal particles. The work includes characterizing particle size distributions, their solubility within the lungs, and the rate at which they are cleared from the respiratory system through stochastic biokinetic modeling. Traditional respiratory biokinetic models typically rely on deterministic assumptions to describe particle transport and retention. In this project, my specific task is to develop stochastic biokinetic/toxicokinetic models from radiological or nuclear and heavy metal intakes as a function of radionuclide inventory, particle size distribution/morphology, solubility, and clearance for expanded inhalation dosimetry quantities.

The enhanced modeling approach will improve our understanding of how inhaled contaminants are transported through the respiratory tract and distributed throughout the body. This information will provide insight into the biological and health effects of these exposures and help evaluate how contaminant retention contributes to lung injury and overall health outcomes in Warfighters.
